Aoraki/Mount Cook and the Tasman Glacier: New Zealand's Highest Peak

At 3,724 metres, Aoraki/Mount Cook is the highest mountain in New Zealand. It stands in the centre of the Southern Alps, in a national park that bears its name, at the head of a broad glacial valley that funnels cold air down from the snowfields above. Below its southeast face, the Tasman Glacier flows for around 27 kilometres toward a lake that formed only in the last few decades as the ice retreated. The entire landscape is geologically young, actively changing, and vast in a way that photographs do not adequately convey.

Aoraki — The Cloud Piercer

New Zealand's highest mountain

3,724 m Aoraki/Mount Cook National Park

Aoraki means "cloud piercer" in Māori — an accurate description of a peak that frequently disappears above cloud cover and creates its own weather. In Ngāi Tahu tradition, Aoraki and his brothers became the South Island's mountains when their canoe ran aground and tilted; the passengers, turned to stone, became the Southern Alps. The mountain was given its dual name — Aoraki/Mount Cook — in 1998 as part of a Treaty of Waitangi settlement with Ngāi Tahu.

The mountain's current height is 3,724 metres — lower than its previous listed height of 3,755 metres after a major rockfall and ice collapse in 1991 removed material from the summit cone. It is still the highest point in New Zealand and one of the most serious mountaineering objectives in the Southern Hemisphere. Edmund Hillary trained on these flanks before his Everest ascent in 1953.

The Tasman Glacier

New Zealand's longest glacier — and a new lake

~27 km long Tasman Lake

The Tasman Glacier flows approximately 27 kilometres from the neve (snow accumulation zone) above to the terminal lake below. It is the longest glacier in New Zealand. Like most of the world's glaciers, it has been retreating for decades: the terminal face has retreated several kilometres since systematic measurement began, and the process has accelerated in recent decades.

Tasman Lake — the lake at the glacier's snout — did not exist 50 years ago. It has formed as the glacier has retreated, fed by meltwater and bounded by lateral moraines. The lake is now several kilometres long and filled with icebergs calved from the glacier face. Boat tours operate on the lake, threading through the ice in small inflatable vessels close to the calving face. It is a landscape mid-transformation.

Hooker Valley Track

10 km return 3 hours

The most accessible walk in the national park is the Hooker Valley Track from Mount Cook Village. The 10km return trail crosses three swing bridges above glacial streams and ends at Hooker Lake, with a direct view across the water to Aoraki's south face. The walk is straightforward, well-formed, and achievable for most reasonably fit visitors — no technical skills required. Hooker Lake also contains icebergs in cooler months.

Weather: Aoraki/Mount Cook National Park generates its own severe weather with little warning. Afternoon thunderstorms, sudden cloud, and high winds are common even in summer. Check the forecast before any walk and carry warm, waterproof gear regardless of the morning conditions. The Department of Conservation visitor centre in Mount Cook Village has current conditions and advice.
