Rotorua Geothermal: Wai-O-Tapu Champagne Pool and Pohutu Geyser

Rotorua sits on one of the most active segments of the Taupo Volcanic Zone, a crack in the Earth's crust running the length of the North Island. The result is a city where steam rises from suburban streets, mud pools bubble in public parks, and two geothermal parks within easy reach of downtown contain features found almost nowhere else on earth. The Champagne Pool at Wai-O-Tapu and the Pohutu Geyser at Te Puia are the two most iconic — entirely different in character, each extraordinary in its own way.

Wai-O-Tapu: The Champagne Pool

What it is

Wai-O-Tapu Thermal Wonderland 30 km south of Rotorua

The Champagne Pool is a large hydrothermal crater thought to have formed in a hydrothermal eruption roughly 900 years ago. The pool is around 65 metres in diameter and around 62 metres deep. The water temperature near the surface is around 74°C — too hot to touch, too hot for most microbial life, though some extremophile bacteria thrive near the cooler edges.

The name comes from the continuous carbon dioxide gas bubbling up from the deep hydrothermal source below — stand at the viewing platform and the entire pool shimmers and fizzes. The turquoise colour of the water is caused by silica particles suspended in solution. The vivid orange-red crust around the rim is a mineral precipitate: as the superheated water cools at the edge, it deposits antimony, arsenic, thallium, and trace amounts of gold and silver. The combination is otherworldly.

Artist's Palette and Devil's Bath

Walking circuit Wai-O-Tapu

The Champagne Pool is the headline, but Wai-O-Tapu's 3km walking circuit passes dozens of other features. The Artist's Palette is a broad silica flat coloured in bands of white, yellow, and pink — different mineral deposits laid down over thousands of years. The Devil's Bath gets its unnatural green from a mix of ferrous salts and sulfur. The Lady Knox Geyser, at the park entrance, is triggered artificially each morning at 10:15am using biodegradable soap, which reduces surface tension and sets off an eruption to around 20 metres.

Te Puia: The Pohutu Geyser

New Zealand's largest active geyser

Whakarewarewa Valley 5 km from central Rotorua

Te Puia sits in the Whakarewarewa geothermal valley, which has been continuously occupied by Māori for centuries and remains an active living village today. The Pohutu Geyser — pohutu means "splashing" in Māori — is New Zealand's largest active geyser, erupting to around 30 metres on most active days. It can erupt multiple times per hour or remain almost continuous; activity varies with rainfall, groundwater levels, and seismic conditions.

The nearby Prince of Wales Feathers geyser typically erupts just before Pohutu, acting as a natural indicator. The geyser field also includes boiling mud pools, silica terraces, and the largest concentration of active geysers in the Southern Hemisphere.

Te Puia beyond the geyser

Māori arts and culture

Te Puia is also the national school of Māori arts and crafts, where master carvers and weavers teach and practise traditional techniques in open workshops that visitors walk through. Watching a tohunga whakairo (master carver) at work is one of the more genuinely educational experiences in Rotorua — the craft takes years to learn and the carvings produced are not for sale. Evening cultural performances with hangi (earth oven) meals are available separately.

Planning tip: Wai-O-Tapu and Te Puia are distinct parks about 20 minutes apart by car. Visiting both in a day is easy — Wai-O-Tapu in the morning (arrive by 9am before the crowds, then stay for the Lady Knox eruption at 10:15am), Te Puia in the afternoon. Allow about two hours for each.
