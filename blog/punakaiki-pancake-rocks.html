Punakaiki Pancake Rocks and Blowholes: The West Coast's Natural Spectacle

On the West Coast of the South Island, where the Tasman Sea meets the Paparoa limestone, a series of rock formations rise from the coastal bluffs in stacked horizontal layers — each layer a few centimetres thick, separated by thin gaps, piled one on top of the other like an enormous stack of pancakes. The effect of differential erosion on 30-million-year-old limestone has produced a coastline that looks designed rather than geological. When a large swell is running and the tide is right, water pushed into sea caves below the rocks escapes through the blowhole cracks in columns of spray.

The Geology

Thirty million years of limestone

Paparoa National Park West Coast, South Island

The rock at Punakaiki formed around 30 million years ago when this part of the South Island was below a shallow sea. The remains of marine organisms — shells, corals, foraminifera — accumulated on the seafloor in alternating layers of limestone and mudstone. The harder limestone layers and the softer layers between them were subsequently uplifted and exposed to erosion. Weather and wave action attack the softer material faster, widening the gaps between the harder layers and creating the distinctive stacked appearance. The process is ongoing — the rocks continue to erode with each storm.

The blowholes

Most active in rough swell High tide

Beneath the rock formations, sea caves have been carved by wave action over centuries. As swells push into the caves, air and water are compressed and forced out through cracks and channels in the rock above. When conditions are right — a significant westerly swell from the Tasman Sea, combined with high tide — the blowholes produce dramatic explosions of spray that can reach several metres in height. In calm conditions the blowholes exhale only gently. The most spectacular blowhole activity happens during storms and high seas, which are also the least comfortable conditions to be on the coastal track — finding a day with moderate swell and a high tide is the practical compromise.

The Dolomite Point circuit

1.2 km loop 30 minutes

The main viewing circuit is the Dolomite Point track — a 1.2km sealed loop that leaves from the car park right on State Highway 6 in Punakaiki village. The track winds through the rock formations, past the primary blowhole viewing platforms and along clifftop edges above the sea. It is manageable for most visitors including those with pushchairs on the sealed sections, though some clifftop sections are exposed and have barriers rather than fences. For a longer experience, the Pororari River Track (10km return) leads into the national park through subtropical forest along the river valley.

On the West Coast road trip: Punakaiki is almost exactly halfway between Westport (45km north) and Greymouth (45km south) on State Highway 6. It is a natural stop on any West Coast transit — the car park and viewing track take about 45 minutes from arrival to departure. Combine with a stop at Hokitika (glowworm dell, pounamu jade shops) to the south, or continue north toward Nelson through the Buller Gorge.
