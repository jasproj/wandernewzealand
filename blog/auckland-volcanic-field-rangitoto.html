Auckland Volcanic Field and Rangitoto Island: The City Built on Volcanoes

Auckland is one of the few cities in the world built on an active volcanic field. Around 50 individual volcanoes underlie the city and its surroundings — erupting at different times over the past 200,000 years, each one producing a new cone, lava field, or crater lake in a different location. The most recent of these eruptions happened roughly 600 years ago, just seven kilometres from the Auckland CBD. It produced Rangitoto Island, the wide symmetrical cone that appears in every Auckland skyline photograph and frames the Hauraki Gulf.

The Auckland Volcanic Field

Around 50 volcanoes, no fixed location

Active field 200,000 years of eruptions

What makes the Auckland Volcanic Field unusual — and scientifically interesting — is its pattern. Unlike a volcano like Ruapehu or Tongariro, which erupt from the same cone repeatedly, Auckland's field produces a new volcano in a new location each time. The magma finds a different path to the surface. This means that if the field erupts again, it will create a new volcano somewhere in the city — geologists and civil defence planners have mapped the probabilities but cannot predict the exact location.

The cones that resulted from past eruptions are now Auckland's parks and viewpoints. Mount Eden (Maungawhau), at 196m, offers the best view of the city from its summit crater. One Tree Hill (Maungakiekie), at 182m, stands in Cornwall Park. North Head (Maungauika) guards the harbour entrance from Devonport. Each has walking tracks to the summit and sweeping harbour views.

Rangitoto Island

New Zealand's youngest and largest shield volcano

~600 years old 259 m summit

Rangitoto erupted from the sea around 600 years ago — a brief eruption period by geological standards, producing a broad, low shield of basalt lava that covers roughly 2,300 hectares. Māori living in the Auckland region witnessed the eruption, and oral tradition records the event. The island's lava fields are still visibly raw — rough, black, and almost treeless in places — though the island has developed a remarkable pohutukawa forest on its lava slopes over the past few centuries.

The summit, at 259 metres, is reached via a 7km return walk from the ferry wharf. The track climbs through pohutukawa and nikau palm forest before ascending the summit ridge on open lava. From the top, the full Hauraki Gulf opens out — the Coromandel Peninsula, Waiheke Island, and the Auckland skyline all visible on a clear day. A lava cave system beneath the surface of the island can also be explored on a short side trip from the summit track.

The view from the city's cones

Free to visit Year-round

Most of Auckland's volcanic cones are public reserves with free access. Mount Eden is about 4km from the CBD and has a car park near the summit. The summit crater — Nga Ipu Kai a Mataaho — is a perfectly preserved bowl that drops about 50 metres below the rim. Walking the crater rim takes about 15 minutes. The panoramic view from the top takes in the full Auckland isthmus with both harbours visible — the Waitemata to the north and the Manukau to the south.

Getting to Rangitoto: Ferries run daily from the Downtown Auckland Ferry Terminal. The crossing takes around 25 minutes. The last afternoon ferry departs mid-afternoon in summer — check the timetable before you go. There is no accommodation on the island, so all visits are day trips. Bring water, sunscreen, and good walking shoes — the lava surface is rough.
