Milford Sound and Fiordland: New Zealand's Most Dramatic Landscape

The Milford Sound road ends at a carpark. Beyond it, sheer walls of dark rock rise out of the water on three sides, veined with waterfalls and overhung with rainforest that clings to near-vertical faces. Mitre Peak — 1,692 metres of fractured granite — stands directly in front of you, rising straight from the sea with no foothills, no approach. Rudyard Kipling called Milford Sound the eighth wonder of the world. The label is disputed, but the landscape is not: there is nothing else quite like it.

What It Is — and Why It Looks That Way

A fiord, not a sound

Fiordland National Park South Island

Milford Sound is technically a fiord: a valley carved by a glacier and subsequently flooded by the sea. A "sound" is normally a river valley, but the misnaming was established early and has never been corrected. The fiord is around 16 kilometres long and up to 400 metres deep. The walls — some rising to around 1,200 metres above the water — are the glacially-scoured faces of the mountain range that formed when New Zealand's tectonic plates collided.

The steep geometry means very little flat land exists anywhere near the water. Milford village — the small settlement at the head of the fiord — is accessible only by road through the Homer Tunnel, or by small plane. There are no towns, no private development, and nowhere to go except on the water or the walking tracks.

Rain and waterfalls

6–8 m annual rainfall Year-round falls

Milford Sound receives some of the highest annual rainfall in New Zealand, typically 6 to 8 metres per year, and occasionally more. The mountains force moist westerly air upward, cooling it rapidly and triggering intense rain. After heavy falls, hundreds of temporary waterfalls cascade down the cliff faces — some lasting only hours. The two permanent falls are Stirling Falls (around 96 metres) and Bowen Falls (around 160 metres), both fed by permanent streams above the glacier-polished rock. Visiting in rain is not a disappointment: the sound doubles in dramatic effect under grey skies and full falls.

The unusual underwater world

Black coral Fiordland crested penguin

The heavy rainfall that feeds Milford Sound's waterfalls creates a thin layer of fresh, tannin-stained water floating on the saltwater below. This freshwater layer filters light and recreates deep-ocean conditions near the surface — black coral, which normally grows at depths of 40 metres or more, grows here at 10 to 15 metres. Fiordland crested penguins nest on rocky shores near the mouth of the fiord and are visible from cruise vessels in season (June to November). New Zealand fur seals are present year-round.

Sandflies: Milford Sound is famous for its sandflies — tiny biting insects that appear as soon as you step out of any enclosed space. They don't transmit disease and the bites resolve within a day, but they are relentless on calm days near the shoreline. Long sleeves, insect repellent, and time spent on the water (where the wind keeps them off) are the practical solutions.

Getting There

Milford Sound is around 4 hours by road from Queenstown — a spectacular drive through the Eglinton Valley and the Homer Tunnel — or accessible by small aircraft from Queenstown and Te Anau. Day tours from Queenstown are the most popular option, though the driving time is significant. Staying overnight in Te Anau and driving in the morning allows an earlier, quieter arrival before the day-tour coaches.
