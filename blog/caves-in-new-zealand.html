Caves in New Zealand: The Complete Guide to Underground Adventures

New Zealand has two of the southern hemisphere's most visited cave systems — the Waitomo glowworm caves in the North Island and the Te Anau glowworm caves in Fiordland — plus a rich belt of limestone country through Nelson-Tasman and the West Coast that holds some of the largest and least-visited cave systems in the southern hemisphere. Caves here range from short guided boat rides through bioluminescent chambers to multi-day technical caving expeditions. This guide covers every cave worth visiting, from the most accessible family attractions to wild self-guided passages, with access notes, difficulty ratings, and safety information for each.

The Waitomo System (North Island)

Waitomo Glowworm Cave

North Island Guided only 45 minutes

The Waitomo Glowworm Cave is the centrepiece of the Waitomo valley and one of New Zealand's defining visitor experiences. The standard tour ends with a silent boat ride through the Cathedral cavern, where thousands of Arachnocampa luminosa glowworms cover the ceiling in a dense constellation of blue-green light. The cave was first formally explored in 1887 by local Māori chief Tāne Tinorau and surveyor Fred Mace. Today it receives hundreds of thousands of visitors annually, making advance booking essential in summer. Our full guide to Waitomo Glowworm Caves covers the tour options in depth, including black water rafting and the adventurous Lost World abseil.

Ruakuri Cave

North Island Guided only 2 hours

Ruakuri is the largest of the three main Waitomo cave attractions, with a 2-hour guided walk covering about 1.6km of passageways. It is distinguished by its active formations — stalactites still growing as mineral-laden water drips through — and by the spiralling entry ramp that descends into the cave without steps, making it one of the most accessible cave walks in New Zealand. Glowworms are present throughout, along with cave wētā, eels in the underground stream, and fossilised moa bones visible in the walls at certain points. The cave is Māori-owned and operated; the name "Ruakuri" means "lair of dogs" and refers to its history as a burial ground, requiring a cultural protocol at entry.

Aranui Cave

North Island Guided only 40 minutes

Aranui is a dry cave — no underground stream — and is therefore much quieter than the Glowworm Cave, with no glowworms but an exceptionally dense collection of stalactite and stalagmite formations. The cave is smaller, the groups are smaller, and the guide commentary focuses on the geology of the formations rather than the bioluminescence. Aranui is typically the least-crowded of the three Waitomo caves and suits visitors who are as interested in limestone geology as in glowworms. It is included in several Waitomo combo tickets. The cave is about 3km from the main Waitomo visitor centre.

South Island Glowworm Caves

Te Anau Glowworm Caves

Fiordland, South Island Boat access only Guided

The Te Anau Glowworm Caves were discovered in 1948 on the western shore of Lake Te Anau — a shore that has no road access and must be reached by a 20-minute boat ride from Te Anau township. The cave system has three main chambers carved by an ancient underground stream, the last of which is navigated by small boat in near-total darkness while the glowworm-covered ceiling reflects in the water below. The cave walls hold fossil-bearing limestone that predates the formation of Fiordland's fiords. Guided tours depart from Te Anau in the afternoon and evening; booking ahead is essential. Te Anau is the gateway town for Milford Sound and Fiordland, making it a natural stop on any fiordland trip.

Wild and Self-Guided Caves

Cave Stream, Arthur's Pass

Canterbury, South Island Self-guided 594m passage

Cave Stream is a 594-metre-long natural tunnel through which a real stream flows — you wade upstream through it, in cold water (typically 7–10°C) that can reach knee to thigh depth. The cave is entirely self-guided, there is no lighting inside, and the passage requires a reliable torch, warm clothes, and footwear that can get wet. The current is manageable in normal conditions but the cave floods rapidly when rain falls in the catchment above, and people have drowned here. Read the safety signs at the entrance every visit, even if you have done the cave before. Do not enter if heavy rain is forecast or recent. The cave is located on State Highway 73 near the Broken River confluence, on the route between Christchurch and the West Coast.

Clifden Caves, Southland

Southland, South Island Self-guided Free

The Clifden Caves are a free self-guided limestone cave complex near Clifden, south of Manapouri in Southland. There are three passages of varying difficulty, from a straightforward walking route to a tighter scrambling passage that requires crawling in places. Torches are essential — there is no installed lighting. The caves are not large by technical standards but they are genuine limestone caves with formations and are one of the few places in New Zealand where you can explore caves entirely on your own without guides or fees. The caves are a short detour off the main Tuatapere–Manapouri road and are signposted from Clifden village.

Nelson, Tasman, and the West Coast Cave Belt

Ngarua Caves, Takaka Hill

Takaka Hill, South Island Guided 45 minutes

Ngarua Caves sit at the top of Takaka Hill on the road between Motueka and Golden Bay — a particularly scenic stretch of highway. The cave is famous for its moa fossils: a significant number of moa bones were found here, and some are displayed in situ within the cave itself. Guided 45-minute tours cover a 200-metre circuit through limestone formations. The tours are seasonal (check current operating times before visiting) and run from the farm property above the cave. Ngarua is a useful stop on the drive from Nelson to Abel Tasman or Farewell Spit. The Abel Tasman and Kahurangi national parks are within an hour's drive.

Honeycomb Hill Caves, Kahurangi

Kahurangi NP, South Island Guided permit required

Honeycomb Hill Caves, in the Oparara Basin near Karamea on the West Coast, is one of New Zealand's most significant cave fossil sites. The deposit contains the largest known concentration of moa bones found together in a single cave, along with the remains of numerous other extinct and extant bird species, making it an irreplaceable paleontological record of pre-human New Zealand. Access is strictly controlled through DOC-approved guided tours to protect the fossil deposit. The Oparara Basin also features impressive natural arches (the Oparara Arch and Moria Gate Arch) visible on a short walk from the car park. Contact DOC or the Karamea i-SITE for current tour availability.

Harwood's Hole, Takaka

Nelson/Tasman, South Island Abseil entry only Expert cavers

Harwood's Hole near Takaka is among the deepest vertical entrance shafts to a cave in New Zealand — the open shaft descends approximately 176 metres before reaching the cave floor. This is strictly a technical caving destination: entry requires a long abseil, expert caving equipment, and a group experienced in cave rescue. It is not a tourist attraction and should not be approached by anyone without proper training and equipment. The cave connects to a longer system within the Takaka Hill limestone, making it significant for New Zealand spelunking. The surface track to the shaft opening passes through native bush and takes about an hour from the road.

Nikau Cave, Waikato

North Island, Waikato Guided 45 minutes

Nikau Cave is a privately operated glowworm cave in the Waikato region, separate from and quieter than the main Waitomo complex. Tour groups are kept small, and the pace is relaxed — the guiding emphasis is on the ecology of the glowworms and the geology of the formations rather than throughput. The cave has active stalactite formations as well as glowworms, and a small number of cave wētā. Because it sees a fraction of Waitomo's visitor numbers, Nikau Cave is a genuinely quieter alternative for people who find the main Waitomo experience too crowded. Nikau trees grow above the cave entrance, giving the property its name.

Guided vs wild caves — the key distinction: All glowworm caves in New Zealand are guided only (the glowworms are fragile and sensitive to disturbance). Wild self-guided caves (Cave Stream, Clifden, Harwood's Hole) require your own equipment, torches, warm layers, and good judgement. For any self-guided cave: always go in a group of at least three, carry two light sources each, tell someone outside your plans, and check flood/weather risk before entering. See DOC's cave safety guidance for NZ-specific advice.

When to Go and How to Get There

The glowworm caves (Waitomo, Te Anau) are open year-round and the glowworms are active every month — temperature inside the caves is stable at around 10–12°C regardless of season. Summer (December–February) is the busiest period; book Waitomo tours at least a few days in advance. The adventurous caves — Cave Stream and Clifden — are best in settled summer weather when river levels are lower, though Cave Stream is potentially accessible year-round in dry periods. Honeycomb Hill and Ngarua are seasonal guided tours; confirm with operators before travel.

Waitomo is about 2 hours south of Auckland and 1.5 hours from Hamilton, easily reached on SH37. Te Anau is the base for all Fiordland exploration and is 2.5 hours from Queenstown on SH94. Cave Stream is directly on SH73 between Christchurch and Greymouth. Clifden Caves are on the road south of Manapouri near Clifden bridge. The Nelson-Tasman cave belt (Ngarua, Harwood's Hole, Honeycomb Hill) is accessed via SH60 from Nelson or the Karamea road from Westport.

What to Bring

For guided commercial caves: warm layers (caves are 10–12°C), comfortable walking shoes. Guided operators provide any additional equipment such as helmets, wetsuits, or harnesses. For self-guided caves: a waterproof torch plus a spare, warm synthetic or wool base layers, footwear that can get completely wet, and in the case of Cave Stream, consider a wetsuit in cold months. Carry a basic first aid kit and a way to communicate your expected return time to someone outside the cave.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you need a guide to visit caves in New Zealand?

It depends on the cave. The Waitomo caves (Glowworm Cave, Ruakuri, Aranui), Te Anau Glowworm Caves, and Honeycomb Hill are guided only. Cave Stream and Clifden Caves are self-guided but require proper equipment and judgment. Harwood's Hole is for experienced technical cavers only. Always check DOC's website and local i-SITEs for current access conditions before visiting.

What creates the glowworm light?

New Zealand glowworms (Arachnocampa luminosa) are the larvae of a fungus gnat endemic to New Zealand and Australia. They produce bioluminescent blue-green light from a modified organ to attract flying insects into their silk trap threads. The species is found only in New Zealand and Australia; the New Zealand species typically produces a brighter and more consistent glow than its Australian relative.

Is Cave Stream safe for families with children?

Cave Stream is suitable for older children (roughly 10+) who are confident in cold water, can follow instructions carefully, and can carry and manage their own torch. It is not suitable for young children, anyone who cannot manage cold water, or anyone in a group without an experienced adult who has read the safety signs at the entrance. The cave should not be entered in flood or high-water conditions under any circumstances.
