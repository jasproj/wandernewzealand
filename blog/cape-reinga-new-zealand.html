Cape Reinga: Where the Spirits Depart

At the northern tip of the Aupōuri Peninsula, a white lighthouse stands on a headland where the Tasman Sea and the Pacific Ocean collide. On clear days you can see the two bodies of water — visually different in colour and wave pattern — churning together in a line of broken water extending from the cape. Below the lighthouse, a lone pōhutukawa tree clings to the cliff face. In Māori tradition, this is Te Rerenga Wairua: the leaping place of spirits, where the souls of the dead begin their journey back to Hawaiki.

The Spiritual Significance

Te Rerenga Wairua

Sacred Māori site Te Aupōuri, Ngāti Kuri

For Māori, Cape Reinga — Te Rerenga Wairua — is where the spirits of the dead travel after leaving the mortal world. The soul departs from the North Island, descends the cliff via the roots of the ancient pōhutukawa below the lighthouse, enters the ocean at the base of the cape, and travels to the Three Kings Islands — Manawatāwhi — before diving beneath the sea on the final journey to Hawaiki, the ancestral homeland from which Polynesian peoples originally set out.

The cape is considered tapu (sacred). Visitors are asked to stay on marked paths and not to eat food near the pōhutukawa or the headland itself. The local iwi (tribes) — primarily Te Aupōuri and Ngāti Kuri — maintain a strong cultural connection to the site and guide its management today.

The Geography

Where two oceans meet

Northland, North Island ~110 km north of Kaitaia

Cape Reinga sits at the northwestern tip of the Aupōuri Peninsula, where the Tasman Sea to the west meets the Pacific Ocean to the east. The junction is visible on calm days as a distinct line where the two different swells converge. On rough days the interaction produces dramatic standing waves and turbulence. The cape is not quite the northernmost point of New Zealand — that distinction belongs to the Surville Cliffs, further east and inaccessible to the public — but it is the northernmost point most New Zealanders and visitors will ever reach.

The pōhutukawa tree

Estimated ~800 years old Protected

A single pōhutukawa grows on the cliff face below the lighthouse, its roots gripping the rock above the sea. It is estimated to be around 800 years old, making it one of the older pōhutukawa specimens in the country, though exact ages for this species are difficult to establish precisely. In summer, pōhutukawa bloom in brilliant red — New Zealand's Christmas tree, flowering through December and January. This particular tree flowers, but its spiritual significance means visitors view it from the path rather than approaching it.

Getting There

The drive north

5.5–6 hrs from Auckland Sealed road to cape

The cape is around 110km north of Kaitaia on a sealed road that narrows as it passes through the Aupōuri Peninsula — a long finger of land between the two coasts, covered in low shrub and tea tree. The drive itself is striking in its emptiness: this is one of the least-populated parts of New Zealand, and the peninsula feels like the edge of the world well before you reach the cape. Day tours from Paihia (Bay of Islands) cover the distance in about 2.5 hours and often drive sections of Ninety Mile Beach — a misnomer at around 88km, but a spectacular stretch of tidal sand that serves as an informal highway.

What to bring: Wind is reliable at Cape Reinga year-round. Even on summer days, the headland is exposed and the temperature on the cape can be 5–10°C cooler than inland. Bring a windproof layer regardless of the forecast. There is a small DOC visitor centre but no petrol station north of Kaitaia, so fill up before you head north.
